Reading The Tarot of Satan: A Beginner's Frame

My own deck, twenty years in the making. How it differs from traditional Tarot, and why its language is stricter.

✦ Shamman Ondieki Master of Black Magic & Witchcraft

I began designing The Tarot of Satan in 2004 and did not finish it for eleven years. Not because the artwork took that long, but because the language did. A Tarot deck is not a set of illustrations — it is a vocabulary, and a vocabulary must be earned before it can be published.

This essay is a frame for those who have acquired the deck, or who are considering it, or who simply want to understand how a working reader thinks about Tarot — particularly a reader whose practice is not oriented toward comfort.

What This Deck Is Not

The Tarot of Satan is not a "dark" Tarot. It is not a shock deck, a horror deck, or a gimmick for people who enjoy occult aesthetics. Anyone looking for pentagrams and goat heads on every card will be disappointed. There is almost nothing visually sensational in it.

Nor is it a re-named Rider-Waite deck. It does not simply redraw the same scenes in a sinister palette and expect you to read them the same way. The structure is different. The card meanings are different. Some cards that traditionally lean toward comfort lean toward confrontation here, and some that lean toward fear lean toward clarity.

The name is not theatrical. Satan in this deck is used in its oldest sense — the adversary, the one who opposes, the tester. Every card in the deck is either a testing, a response to testing, or the consequence of one.

The Structure

Traditional Tarot is built on a 78-card structure: 22 Major Arcana, 56 Minor Arcana divided into four suits. The Tarot of Satan keeps the count but changes the architecture.

  • 22 Major Arcana — but the sequence is reordered, and three traditional figures are replaced. The Fool is not the same Fool. The Devil is not the same Devil. The World is not arrived at; it is departed from.
  • Four suits, renamed — Blade, Chain, Vessel, and Coin. They correspond loosely to Swords, Wands, Cups, and Pentacles, but each carries an additional valence that the traditional suits do not.
  • Court cards — Page, Knight, Queen, King remain, but their meanings are shifted toward function rather than personality. The Queen of Vessels is not "a nurturing woman." She is the person who holds — regardless of gender, age, or temperament.

This is not a change made for novelty. It is a change made because the traditional structure was developed by and for a certain kind of reader in a certain kind of context — and that context is not the one I work in.

The Language Is Stricter

This is the most important thing to understand about the deck. Traditional Tarot is forgiving. If you draw the Three of Swords, the deck allows you to read it as heartbreak, as clarity, as surgery, as truth-telling. You have room to soften the meaning if the querent needs it.

The Tarot of Satan does not allow this. Each card in this deck speaks a single meaning, expressed in three forms — the surface reading, the middle reading, and the root reading. If the card says heartbreak, it says heartbreak. You may tell the querent what the heartbreak is about, but you may not tell them it is not heartbreak. The deck will correct you on the next throw.

A strict deck serves the question.

How to Begin

If you have acquired the deck, and you intend to use it, the first months should be spent without clients. Sit with one card a day. Not to memorize — to listen. The cards have opinions. They will tell you what they are, if you do not impose meanings from other decks.

Some specific advice:

  1. Do not use the Little White Book first. The deck ships with a short guide, but read it last. First, sit with the images. Write down what each one says before you consult the guide. If your reading matches, you are ready to work. If it does not, sit longer.
  2. Do not read for others until you have read for yourself for six months. A strict deck will embarrass an unprepared reader. The embarrassment is instructive, but it should not happen in front of a client.
  3. Do not ask yes-or-no questions. This deck does not answer them. It answers questions about structure, relationship, and cost. If your question has no cost, ask a different question.
  4. Do not "cleanse" the deck between readings. This is a habit imported from other traditions and it does not apply here. The deck accumulates. Let it.

What the Deck Is For

This is a working deck. It is used in my practice for diagnosis — determining the nature of a case before I accept it. It is faster and more honest than most divination tools for that purpose, and it does not flatter.

Clients sometimes ask whether I will read their cards at the start of a consultation. I do not. I read the deck silently, before they speak, to determine whether the case is what they believe it is. Only after that do I ask my diagnostic questions. This is not a courtesy — it is a safeguard. It prevents the querent's desire from contaminating the reading.

If the deck and the querent disagree, I tell them so. If the deck and I disagree, I sit with it again and let it correct me. This is how the deck remains strict.

For Those Who Do Not Have the Deck

Many who write to me will never acquire the deck, and that is fine. It is not the only deck, nor the only path, nor a requirement for working with me. What I want to leave you with is the principle underneath it: a working deck must have a language. If your deck says whatever you want it to say, it is not a tool — it is a mirror. And a mirror will not tell you what you do not already know.

If you are looking for a mirror, use any deck. If you are looking for a witness, choose your deck carefully and let it speak.
