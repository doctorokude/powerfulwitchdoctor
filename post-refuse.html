When to Refuse a Request

Twenty years in, I still turn away certain cases. A short essay on the boundaries of this work, and why I hold them.

✦ Shamman Ondieki Master of Black Magic & Witchcraft

Every practitioner of consequence has a boundary — a line past which he will not work. The line is not moral. It is not the line between "good" and "evil," because those words describe the client's intent, not the work. The line is somewhere else.

I have refused cases that would have paid me a year's income. I have taken cases that paid almost nothing. The reasons have never been about money. They have always been about whether the work could be done correctly, and whether I would be able to look at the result afterwards without regret.

This essay is about that line. If you are considering writing to me, it may save you time. If your case falls on the wrong side of it, I will tell you — but you will already know.

The First Refusal: The Client Who Wants to Watch

There is a category of inquiry that begins with the words "Can I see it happen?" or "Can I be present when you do it?" or "Will you send me a video?"

I refuse these immediately. Not because they are disrespectful — many come from genuine curiosity — but because the desire to watch is a sign that the client does not understand what the work is.

Magical work is not a performance. It is not a demonstration. It is not a thing that can be photographed, filmed, or witnessed by an audience. If it could be, it would not be magic — it would be theater.

Clients who want to watch are clients who want to be reassured that something is happening. That reassurance is the opposite of the work, because the work requires the client's trust without evidence. If I have to prove the work is real before it is done, the work will fail — for the same reason that a man who keeps checking whether his wife loves him will eventually be told she does not.

The Second Refusal: The Client Who Wants Two Outcomes

This is subtler and much more common. A client writes to me and describes a situation where they want two contradictory things, and they want me to deliver both.

"Bring him back to me, but if he doesn't want to come back, make him suffer."

"Make my business succeed, but also make my competitor fail."

"Protect me, but let me also get revenge."

These are not single requests. They are two requests, each pulling in an opposite direction, and the client has not yet decided which they want. The magical work cannot be done in this state, because the current has no destination. Whatever is sent out will be split, weakened, and ultimately returned to the client as confusion.

I do not judge the client for this. I tell them so, and I tell them to write again when they have decided. Many do. Some never do — and that is the honest answer to their situation, not a failure on my part.

The Third Refusal: The Client Who Wants to Punish

This is the hardest category to describe without sounding sanctimonious, so let me be precise. I do not refuse cases because the client is angry, or because the outcome they seek is difficult for their enemy. I refuse cases where the purpose of the work is the enemy's suffering, and there is no other purpose.

There is a difference between:

  • "I want to be protected from this person" — a legitimate request.
  • "I want this person to lose the power to harm me" — a legitimate request.
  • "I want this person to suffer the way they made me suffer" — not a legitimate request.

The first two are structural. The work has a shape, a beginning, and an end. Once the protection is in place or the harm is neutralized, the work is complete.

The third is infinite. There is no "done." There is only more suffering to extract. And any practitioner who takes such a case on will find himself doing it again, and again, and again, until the client's fury has consumed everything — including the practitioner.

Justice has an end.
Revenge does not.

The Fourth Refusal: The Client Who Will Not Name the Case

Occasionally a client writes to me and asks for help, but refuses to describe the situation. They want the work done "in general." They want protection from "something." They want a "cleansing" without saying what needs to be cleansed.

This always fails. Not sometimes — always. The reason is simple: magic requires a target. A general protection is not a protection at all; it is a hope. A general cleansing is not a cleansing; it is a bath.

When a client cannot or will not name their case, it usually means one of three things: they do not yet know what they want, they are ashamed of what they want, or they are hiding something from me. All three make the work impossible — the first because there is nothing to work on, the second because the current will resist the target, and the third because every hidden detail will sabotage the ritual from within.

I tell these clients to write again when they are ready to speak plainly. Many are ready within days. Some are never ready — and that, too, is an honest answer.

The Fifth Refusal: The Client I Cannot Help

This is the most important refusal, and the hardest to make, because it is not about the client's intent or the client's choice. It is about me.

There are cases where I look at the situation and know, with the certainty that comes from twenty years of practice, that I am not the right practitioner for this work. Not because the case is beneath me. Not because the client has done something to offend. Simply because the work requires a practitioner with a different relationship to the unseen than mine.

In such cases I refuse, and I tell the client why. I often suggest one or two other practitioners who might be better suited. This is not a courtesy. It is a discipline. To take a case I cannot complete, out of pride or greed or a wish to seem capable, is to harm the client, the work, and myself.

Over twenty years, I have learned that my reputation is not built on the cases I accept. It is built on the cases I refuse, and on the reasons I give for refusing them.

What I Always Accept

For balance, it may help to state what I always accept, regardless of complexity:

  • Protection for a person in genuine danger — always.
  • Removal of negative magical influence — always.
  • Diagnosis — always. Even if the diagnosis is "this is not magical at all."
  • Honest counsel — always, whether or not the case proceeds.
  • Refusal with explanation — always. No one who writes to me seriously leaves without a real answer, even when the answer is no.

How to Write to Me

If, after reading this, you are still unsure whether your case falls on the right side of the line, write anyway. Describe your situation plainly. Say what you want, and why you want it. Do not try to make the request sound better than it is. Do not hide the parts you are ashamed of.

I will read what you send. I will tell you honestly whether I will take the case, why I will or will not, and if I will not, what alternatives exist.

That is the promise of this practice. It is the only promise I make. It is the only promise worth making.

— Shamman Ondieki
